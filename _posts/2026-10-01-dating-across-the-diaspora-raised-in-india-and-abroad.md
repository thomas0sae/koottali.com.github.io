One of the most common pairings in NRI dating looks like this: one person moved
abroad in their twenties for study or work, and the other was born or raised in
the country where they now both live.

From the outside, they are two Indians who met abroad. From the inside, they
often grew up with quite different ideas of what normal looks like, and the
differences show up in places neither of them expected.

None of this makes the relationship harder to get right than any other. It just
helps to know where to look.

## Shared, and not quite shared

You probably share more than other people assume. The food, the festivals, the
films, the family group chats, the particular way relatives ask about your
career. There is a lot that needs no explanation.

But you may also assume you share things you do not. One person's idea of how
often you call your parents, how a household runs or what a wedding involves was
formed in Pune or Kochi. The other's was formed in New Jersey, Leicester or
Dubai, as the child of parents who were themselves adapting to a new country.
Both versions are Indian. They are not the same version.

## Where the differences tend to show up

### Family closeness and decision-making

Someone raised in India may be used to parents being involved in decisions large
and small, and may see that involvement as care. Someone raised abroad may have
grown up with more distance by default, or may have spent years negotiating
independence from parents who were stricter than their friends' parents. The same
phone call from a mother can feel warm to one partner and intrusive to the other.

### Language and humour

Fluency in a mother tongue often differs. One partner may follow every joke at a
family dinner while the other catches half of it and smiles along, which can feel
lonelier than it looks. The reverse happens too, in the country where you live,
with its own references, slang and unspoken rules.

### Money and obligations

Many people who moved abroad as adults support parents or siblings back home, or
expect to as their parents age. Someone raised abroad may have grown up in a
family where money flowed the other way, or where those obligations were less
explicit. Neither is wrong, but the difference needs to be said out loud before
it is discovered in a bank statement.

### Roles at home

Expectations about who cooks, who manages the house and who looks after visiting
parents are shaped early and by example. Couples are often surprised by which
assumptions they turn out to hold.

### Ideas of home

For one of you, India may be home in a literal sense: a place you might return
to. For the other, it may be somewhere you visit and love but would never live.
That affects where you buy a house, where you want children to grow up and where
you picture yourselves in your sixties. Our guide to

covers the decisions this can lead to.

### Religion and festivals in practice

Two people from the same faith can practise it very differently, depending on
whether it was the background of daily life or something families held onto more
deliberately in a new country. Our post on
[interfaith and inter-caste relationships]({{ '/interfaith-and-inter-caste-relationships-in-the-diaspora/' | relative_url }})
covers conversations that apply here too.

## Drop the labels

ABCD, FOB, coconut, "too Indian", "not Indian enough". These labels get thrown
around as jokes, but they flatten real people into stereotypes, and they tend to
sting more than the person using them realises.

They are also usually wrong. People raised abroad are not necessarily less
traditional, and people raised in India are not necessarily more conservative.
Some of the most rigid expectations come from families who emigrated decades ago
and held onto the India they left. Ask about someone's actual experience instead.
It is far more interesting than the stereotype.

## What each of you brings

Couples who make this work tend to treat the mix as a strength.

The partner raised in India often brings a living connection to the culture, the
language and the extended family, along with the resilience of someone who has
already rebuilt a life once.

The partner raised abroad often brings fluency in the country you share, its
systems and its social life, and a hard-won understanding of what it is like to
grow up between two cultures. That understanding is exactly what your children
will need, if you have them.

## Making it work

**Get specific early.** "How often would you want to see your parents?" is a
better question than "are you close to your family?" Specific questions surface
real differences while they are still easy to talk about.

**Spend time in each other's worlds.** Go to India together, ideally including
the town or city your partner grew up in, not just the round of family weddings.
Spend time with your partner's friends from school or university, not only their
relatives.

**Be patient at family gatherings.** If your partner is lost in a language or a
set of customs, translate, explain and include them. If you are the one who is
lost, ask. Most families are delighted to be asked.

**Agree how you will handle family together.** Decide what gets discussed with
parents and what stays between you, and present a united front on the things you
have agreed. Our guides to
[talking to Indian parents about dating]({{ '/talking-to-indian-parents-about-dating/' | relative_url }})
and
[meeting their parents for the first time]({{ '/meeting-their-parents-for-the-first-time/' | relative_url }})
cover the early stages.

**Talk about children before they arrive.** Which languages they will speak, how
much time they will spend in India, how religion and festivals will feature.
These are easy to agree in principle and surprisingly hard to agree in detail.

## The short version

If one of you grew up in India and the other abroad, expect to share more than
outsiders think and less than you assume. The differences tend to show up in
family involvement, money, roles at home, language and the idea of where home is.
Drop the labels, ask specific questions early, spend real time in each other's
worlds, and agree on family matters as a couple.

The couples who do this well are not the ones with the most in common. They are
the ones who stay curious about the parts they do not share.
