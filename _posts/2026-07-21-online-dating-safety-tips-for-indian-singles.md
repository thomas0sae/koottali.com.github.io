Most people you meet on a dating app are exactly who they say they are. But the
minority who are not can do real damage, and a handful of habits remove most of
the risk without making dating joyless.

This is the practical version — what to watch for, what to withhold, and how to
handle a first meeting.

## Protect your personal information

### What to keep back at first

Some information is fine to share early. Your first name, your general area, what
you do broadly, your interests — all normal.

Some information should wait until you have met and trust someone:

- Your home address, or a photo that clearly identifies your building
- Your specific workplace and your working hours
- Financial details of any kind, including how much you earn or have saved
- Aadhaar, passport, PAN, national insurance or social security numbers
- Intimate images

That last one deserves emphasis. Once an image is sent, you no longer control it.
Sextortion — where someone obtains intimate images and then threatens to send
them to family or employers — specifically targets people who have a lot to lose
socially, and it is a known pattern against members of tightly connected
communities.

### Watch what your photos reveal

Background details identify locations more often than people expect: a house
number, a nameplate, a college hoodie, a metro station, a car registration, the
view from your window.

You do not need to be paranoid. Just glance at the background before uploading.

### Keep conversation on the app at first

Dating apps have reporting and blocking that works, and a record of the
conversation. Someone pushing hard to move to WhatsApp or Telegram in the first
few messages is worth noting — it is the standard opening move for scam accounts,
because it removes the app's protections.

Moving to another channel later, once you have a sense of someone, is completely
normal.

## Spot fake profiles and romance scams

### Signals worth taking seriously

**They will not do a live video call.** This is the single most reliable test. A
real person will hop on a short video call. Scam accounts produce an endless
supply of reasons why not — broken camera, poor connection, offshore work,
military deployment. Affection over text combined with persistent refusal to
appear live is close to conclusive.

**Very few photos, all unusually polished.** Stolen images tend to come from a
model or influencer account. Run one through a reverse image search if something
feels off.

**Intensity arriving far too early.** Declarations of love within days, talk of
marriage before you have met, constant messaging that escalates quickly. Rushed
emotional intimacy is a deliberate technique — it is designed to make later
requests harder to refuse.

**Details that drift.** The city changes. The job changes. The sibling has a
different name than last week. Scam accounts run many conversations at once and
lose track.

**A story engineered for distance.** Working on an oil rig, deployed overseas,
a surgeon with an international charity. Occupations that plausibly explain why
they can never meet or video call should raise your guard, not lower it.

**Any request for money.** This is the point of the entire exercise. It arrives
as an emergency — a medical bill, a customs charge on a gift, a stranded traveller,
a business opportunity that closes today. It may start small to establish the
pattern.

Never send money to someone you have not met in person. There is no version of
this that ends well.

### Investment and crypto scams

A newer variant does not ask for money directly. Instead the conversation drifts
towards how well they are doing with crypto or forex trading, and eventually an
offer to show you the platform they use.

The platform is fabricated. Small withdrawals are permitted early to build
confidence. Larger deposits cannot be withdrawn. If someone you met on a dating
app is teaching you to invest, that is the scam.

## Meeting in person for the first time

### Video call first

A short video call before meeting confirms the person matches their photos and
gives you a sense of them that text does not. It is now a normal request and
nobody reasonable objects.

### Meet in public, in daylight if you can

A café, a busy restaurant, a park. Not their home, not yours, not somewhere
isolated. Keep the first meeting short — an hour is plenty — so it is easy to
leave if it is not working.

### Arrange your own transport

Get there and back under your own steam. Do not be picked up from home, and do not
let a first date end with them knowing your address.

### Tell someone

Give a friend the person's name, their profile, where you are going and when you
expect to be back. Share your live location if your phone supports it. Arrange a
check-in message.

### Watch your drink

Do not leave a drink unattended, and do not accept one you did not see poured.
This applies regardless of gender.

### Leave if you want to leave

You owe no one an explanation, a full evening, or politeness in the face of
discomfort. If something feels wrong, go. Trusting that instinct costs nothing if
you are mistaken.

## Pressure is a red flag in itself

Beyond scams, watch how someone responds to a boundary. Early pressure to move
faster than you want — physically, emotionally, or towards sharing
information — tells you something important, and it rarely improves later.

Someone who accepts "not yet" gracefully is showing you how they will handle
disagreement for the rest of the relationship. So is someone who does not.

## If something goes wrong

**If you have been scammed financially,** report it to your bank immediately —
some transfers can be stopped — and to your national cybercrime service. In India
that is the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or the 1930
helpline. Do not let embarrassment stop you; these operations are professional
and target thousands of people.

**If you are being threatened with images,** do not pay. Payment reliably leads to
further demands. Preserve the messages, report the account, and contact the
police. Support organisations exist for exactly this and have handled it many
times before.

**If someone has harmed you,** it was not your fault, and reporting it helps
protect other people.

## The short version

Keep identifying details back until you have met. Insist on a video call. Never
send money. Meet in public, on your own transport, having told a friend. Leave
whenever you want.

None of this makes dating grim. It just means the small number of people acting
in bad faith run out of road early, and you get on with meeting the many who are
not.
