Most profiles fail for boring reasons. Not because the person is unremarkable,
but because the first photo is a group shot, the bio says "love travelling and
good food," and there is nothing on the screen that another person could
reasonably reply to.

The good news is that this is all fixable in about twenty minutes. Here is what
actually matters, roughly in order of how much difference it makes.

## Your photos do most of the work

People decide on photos first and read the bio second, if at all. Getting the
photos right is the single highest-return change you can make.

### The first photo

One person — you — with your face clearly visible, well lit, and no sunglasses.
That is the whole rule.

The most common mistake by a distance is opening with a group photo. Nobody
wants to work out which one you are, so they swipe. Save group shots for later
in the set, or leave them out entirely.

### The rest of the set

Aim for four to six photos that each do a different job:

- **A clear head-and-shoulders shot** so people know what you look like
- **A full-length photo**, because leaving it out is noticed and assumed about
- **You doing something you genuinely do** — cooking, hiking, at a match, on
  stage, at a desk you are proud of
- **Something with context** — a trip, a festival, a family occasion, your city

Variety matters more than quality. Six well-lit photos of you in the same pose
against the same wall tell someone less than four ordinary ones taken in four
different parts of your life.

### What to leave out

Heavy filters, anything where your face is obscured, photos that are visibly
years old, and screenshots of other photos. Each of these creates doubt, and
doubt is what makes people move on.

## Write a bio someone can reply to

The purpose of a bio is not to summarise your entire personality. It is to give
somebody an obvious opening line.

### The parts that work

A useful bio usually has three things in it:

1. **Something concrete about your life** — what you do, where you are, where
   you are from
2. **Something specific you enjoy**, named precisely rather than generally
3. **What you are looking for**, stated plainly

Specificity is the whole trick. "Love food" gives nobody anything. "Still
looking for a filter coffee in this city that comes close to home" gives
everybody something.

Compare these:

> Easy-going guy, love travelling, food and music. Looking for someone
> genuine.

> Software engineer in Frankfurt, from Kochi originally. Run every Sunday
> morning along the river, cook far more than one person needs to, and I am
> slowly learning German from my neighbours. Looking for something serious.

The second one is not better written. It is just answerable — there are four
different things in it a stranger could ask about.

### Say what you want

If you are looking for marriage rather than something casual, write it. This is
the most effective filter you have, and it costs you nothing except the people
who were never going to work out.

Frame it as direction, not a deadline. "Looking for something serious" reads
well. "Want to be married within a year" reads as pressure, even to someone who
also wants to be married within a year.

### What to cut

- Lists of what you do not want. A bio of negatives reads as a person carrying
  a grudge from their last few conversations.
- "Ask me anything." It transfers all the work to the other person.
- Height, salary and job title stacked up with nothing else around them.
- Sarcasm about being on a dating app. Everyone on it is on it.

## Widen your filters before you rewrite anything

If you are getting very few profiles shown to you, the problem may not be your
profile at all.

People routinely set an age range of five years, a distance of 25km and a
single home state, then conclude the app is empty. In a city with a modest
Indian population, those three filters together can reduce the pool to almost
nothing.

## A twenty-minute profile audit

Go through this in order and stop when you run out of time. The list is ranked.

1. Replace your first photo with a clear, well-lit solo shot of your face
2. Get to at least four photos, each showing something different
3. Rewrite the bio to three to five lines with at least two specific details
4. Add a plain line about what you are looking for
5. Fill in every structured field — languages, home state, interests,
   profession
6. Complete verification
7. Widen your distance filter, then your age range
8. Read the whole thing back and remove anything that could have been written
   by anybody else

Then leave it alone for a fortnight before judging it. Changing a profile every
two days makes it impossible to tell what worked.

## The underlying point

A good profile is not a sales pitch. It is an honest, specific description of a
person, written so that the right stranger has an easy way in — and so that the
wrong ones can tell, quickly, that it is not for them.

Both halves of that matter. A profile that appeals to everybody has usually
been sanded down until it describes nobody.
