At some point, most long-distance couples reach the same question: one of you is
going to have to move.

It is the decision that gets talked about least well, partly because it arrives
wrapped in romance and partly because the practical parts are unglamorous. But
the couples who struggle after a move almost never struggle because they were
wrong about each other. They struggle because nobody worked out what the first
year would actually look like.

This is the conversation to have before anyone books anything.

## Deciding who moves

### Settle it on practicalities, not fairness

Couples often try to resolve this as a question of who loves whom more, or whose
turn it is. That produces resentment and no answer.

The useful version runs through the constraints:

- **Whose work transfers?** Some professions move across borders easily.
  Others - law, medicine, teaching, accountancy, anything requiring local
  licensing - can mean years of requalification or a serious step down.
- **Whose immigration route is simpler?** This varies enormously by country,
  passport and relationship status, and it often decides the question on its own.
- **Who has obligations at home?** Ageing parents, a family business, a sibling
  who depends on you. These are real and they are not always negotiable.
- **Where do you both want to be in ten years?** A move made as a stopgap that
  quietly becomes permanent is a common source of long-term unhappiness.

Write the answers down separately and then compare. People are often more
flexible on paper than they are in conversation, where positions harden.

### Get proper advice on the legal side

One clear warning: immigration rules change constantly, differ by country and
by individual circumstance, and are the single most common source of expensive
mistakes.

Nothing you read online - including this - is a substitute for advice from a
qualified immigration adviser in the destination country. Get it early, before
you build plans on an assumption, and budget for it.

## The conversations to have before the move

### What the first six months look like

The moving partner is giving up, simultaneously: a job, a social circle,
proximity to family, and a working knowledge of how ordinary things are done.
That is four losses at once, and the last one is more draining than people
expect.

Agree in advance what they will actually do. Are they job-hunting from day one,
studying, permitted to work at all under their visa? The gap between "we will
figure it out" and a concrete plan is where most of the misery lives.

### Money, while the balance is uneven

For some period, one of you may be earning and the other not. This needs
discussing explicitly and unromantically before it happens.

- Does the earning partner's income become shared, or does the other person draw
  from savings?
- Does the person not earning have money they can spend without asking?
- What is the budget for flights home, and is it protected rather than the first
  thing cut?
- What continuing obligations does each of you have to family back home?

The dynamic to avoid is one partner feeling they have to justify ordinary
spending. It corrodes things quickly, and it is entirely preventable by deciding
the rule in advance.

### What happens if it does not work

This is the conversation nobody wants and everybody should have.

If the relationship ends six months after one of you has moved countries, what
happens? Does the moving partner have the means to go home? Is their visa status
tied to the relationship, and what does that mean in practice?

Having this conversation is not a lack of faith. It is the difference between a
person who can leave a relationship that has gone wrong and a person who is
trapped in one, and it is worth an uncomfortable hour.

### Families, on both sides

A move usually shifts the family balance permanently - one set of parents gains
proximity and the other loses it, often for good.

Talk about how often you will visit, who hosts whom, and what happens when a
parent's health changes. For many NRI couples, the eventual question of elderly
parents moving in, or one of you returning for an extended period, is the single
biggest structural issue in the marriage. Better raised now than at a crisis.

## Test it properly first

Short visits are misleading. A long weekend shows you two people on their best
behaviour with nothing to do but enjoy each other.

If you possibly can, spend several continuous weeks in the destination city
before committing - ideally with the moving partner doing ordinary things:
commuting, shopping, being bored on a Wednesday, spending time with the other
person's friends without it being an event.

The questions to answer honestly during that time: could you live in this city if
the relationship were not part of it? Do you like who your partner is in their
own environment, among their own people? Are you comfortable with the version of
them that exists when they are stressed and not hosting you?

## After the move

A few things that most couples are not warned about.

**The hard part comes after the novelty.** The first few weeks are usually good.
Months two to six are where it bites - the admin, the loneliness, the absence of
anyone to meet for coffee at short notice.

**The moving partner needs their own life, urgently.** Not derived from yours.
Their own friends, their own routine, something they do without you. A partner
whose entire world is one person is under a weight neither of you chose.

**The settled partner has to do more of the invisible work** - the bank, the
doctor, the paperwork, the explaining - and should expect that without
resenting it.

**Expect six to twelve months of feeling unsettled**, and treat that as normal
rather than as evidence of a mistake. Almost everyone reports it.

**Protect the trips home.** They are not a luxury and they are not optional.
When money is tight they are the first thing to be cut, and cutting them is
usually a false economy.

## The short version

Decide who moves on the basis of careers, visas and family obligations rather
than on who is more committed. Get professional immigration advice early. Agree
what the first six months look like, how money works while it is uneven, and
what happens if it ends. Spend real time in the destination before committing.
Then expect the first year to be harder than the visits were.

Couples who do this well are not the ones who felt most certain. They are the
ones who were specific.
