In a lot of cultures, meeting the parents is a casual step. In most Indian
families it is not - it is closer to an announcement. The invitation itself says
that your partner considers this serious, and everyone in the room knows it.

That raises the stakes, but it also makes the whole thing more predictable than
people expect. There is a fairly standard script, and knowing it removes most of
the anxiety.

## Before you go

### Get briefed properly

Ask your partner for the specifics, and ask for more detail than feels necessary:

- **Names and relationships.** Who will be there, and what you should call each
  of them. Getting an uncle's name right is a small thing that lands well.
- **The household's rules.** Vegetarian or not, alcohol or not, shoes off at the
  door, whether Friday or a particular day matters.
- **What they already know about you.** Whether your religion, caste, marital
  status, divorce or children have been mentioned. Walking into a room where
  something significant has been withheld is the single worst version of this
  meeting.
- **The subjects to leave alone.** Every family has two or three. Politics, a
  particular relative, a business that failed.

If your partner is vague about any of this, press gently. Vagueness before a
first meeting usually means something has not been said yet, and it is far
better to know in advance.

### What to bring

Something modest and thoughtful beats something expensive. Sweets, good fruit, a
box of something from your own region, flowers if the household likes them.

Avoid anything extravagant. A large gift at a first meeting reads as an attempt
to buy approval and puts the family in the awkward position of owing you
something. If in doubt, ask your partner, and follow their answer exactly.

### How to dress

Slightly more formal than you would normally be, and more conservative than your
usual. This is not about conforming to anything - it is that the first meeting is
the one occasion where being visibly respectful is doing real work, and it costs
you nothing.

## The meeting itself

### The standard questions

You will almost certainly be asked about your family, where your parents live and
what they do, your education, your work, and - depending on the household -
whether you cook.

These are not an interrogation, even when they arrive in rapid succession. They
are how an older generation opens a conversation with a stranger: establishing
context. Answer briefly, without a résumé, and turn it back.

The single most effective thing you can do is **ask them about themselves**.
Where they grew up, when they moved, what the early years abroad were like, how
they met. Most parents are rarely asked these questions and will talk warmly for
half an hour. People leave that conversation liking you considerably more than
they would have after hearing about your promotion.

### Feeding you is the point

You will be given a great deal of food, and then offered more. Accept it, eat it,
and praise it specifically - not "this is lovely" but "this is the best avial I
have had outside Kerala."

In many Indian households feeding a guest is the primary way affection is
expressed, particularly by a parent who is not going to say anything
affectionate out loud. Refusing food, or eating very little, is read more
personally than most guests realise.

If you have a dietary restriction or an allergy, have your partner mention it
beforehand rather than declining at the table.

### What to leave alone

At a first meeting, keep away from:

- **Marriage timelines**, unless they raise it and your partner is happy to
  discuss it
- **Politics and religion**, in any direction
- **Physical affection** with your partner in front of the parents - this reads
  differently in many Indian households than it does in the country you both
  live in
- **Disagreeing with a parent's opinion**, even a mild one, on a first visit

None of this is permanent. It is the first meeting, and the aim is a good
impression rather than an honest debate about farm policy.

### If you are not Indian, or not from the same background

You will probably be asked about it, sometimes directly and sometimes through a
proxy question about your family. The best posture is straightforward, warm and
unapologetic - curious about their traditions rather than defensive about your
own.

A small amount of preparation goes a long way: knowing what the festival coming
up is, how to greet an elder, whether to touch feet in that particular family.
Ask your partner, and do it naturally rather than performing it.

## The two of you

This is the part that actually determines the outcome, and it happens before and
after rather than during.

**Agree in advance what you are presenting.** How serious are you saying this is?
Are marriage or timelines on the table, or not yet? Two people giving different
answers to the same question is what turns a warm meeting into a tense one.

**Your partner handles their own family.** If a parent says something rude, it is
their job to manage it, not yours. Your job is to remain pleasant. A partner who
leaves you to defend yourself in their parents' house is telling you something
important about how the next ten years would go.

**Do not debrief in the car with the windows down.** Wait until you are properly
alone. Then be honest with each other about how it went.

## If it goes badly

A cool reception is common and far less predictive than it feels in the moment.
Parents are meeting someone who represents a large change in their child's life,
and first reactions are rarely final ones.

What works is repetition rather than persuasion: more low-pressure contact,
short visits, small gestures, being present at things that matter to them. Most
families soften over months, once the abstraction has become a person who turns
up.

What does not work is an ultimatum in the first month, or trying to win an
argument you were never going to win in a single evening. Our guide to

## The short version

Get briefed thoroughly, bring something modest, dress up slightly, ask them more
questions than you answer, eat what you are given, and stay off the three or four
subjects that can only cost you.

Agree with your partner beforehand what you are both saying, and let them handle
their own family in the room.

Most of these meetings go fine. The ones that do not usually go wrong for
avoidable reasons - something not disclosed in advance, or two people telling
different stories about how serious they are.
