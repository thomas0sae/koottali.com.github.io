Most dating app conversations do not fail dramatically. They just thin out —
shorter replies, longer gaps, and then nothing. Nobody was rude and nothing went
wrong; the conversation simply ran out of anything to be about.

This is mostly a mechanics problem, and mechanics can be fixed.

## The first message

### Why "hi" does not work

It is not that "hi" is offensive. It is that it puts the entire burden of
starting a conversation on the person receiving it, who very likely has several
other messages saying exactly the same thing.

The fix is small: give them something to reply to.

### What to send instead

The reliable formula is one specific detail from their profile plus an open
question about it. That is it.

> You are from Thrissur and living in Melbourne — did you move for work or
> study? I did the same thing in reverse, sort of.

> A whole photo dedicated to a dosa. Home recipe or is there somewhere here
> that does it properly?

Both are short. Both prove you read the profile. Both are much easier to answer
than to ignore.

If their profile genuinely gives you nothing — and that happens — a light
question about something you have in common still beats a bare greeting. Same
city, same home state, same degree, same job in a different country.

### What to avoid at the start

- **Compliments about appearance.** Everyone gets these and they lead nowhere.
  A compliment about a choice they made — what they cooked, where they went,
  what they are learning — lands differently.
- **Long opening messages.** Six lines before a reply reads as intense, not
  invested.
- **Interview questions.** Age, salary, marriage plans and family details in
  the first exchange feel like a form rather than a conversation, even between
  two people who both want to marry.
- **Anything you have sent word for word to ten other people.** It is more
  obvious than it feels.

## Keeping it going

### Ask open questions, and answer with more than you were asked

Closed questions kill conversations. "Do you like cooking?" can be answered with
"yes" and then you are both stuck. "What is the one thing you always end up
cooking when you cannot be bothered?" cannot.

The other half of this is answering generously. If someone asks where you are
from, do not only name the place — say something about it. Every answer is a
chance to hand the other person a new thread to pull.

### Match their energy

If they are sending three lines, do not send twelve. If they reply once a day,
do not send four messages into the silence. Conversations work when the effort
is roughly symmetrical, and the fastest way to make someone uncomfortable is to
be visibly more invested than they are.

The reverse is also true. If someone is putting real effort into their messages
and getting "haha nice" back, they will stop. If you are interested, show it.

### Talk about things, not about the app

"How is your day going" three days running is the conversational equivalent of
treading water. Pick something real — the city you are both in, a film, the
strangeness of your first winter abroad, what your family thinks you should be
doing with your life.

Mild disagreement is fine and often helps. Two people politely agreeing about
everything produces a conversation neither will remember.

### Do not let it drift for weeks

Texting forever is comfortable and goes nowhere. It also carries a specific
risk: the longer you talk without a call or a meeting, the more you are getting
to know an idea of a person rather than the person.

A few days to a fortnight is usually about right before suggesting a call.

## Moving off the app

### Suggest the call before you need to

"Fancy a quick video call this week?" is not a big ask, and how someone responds
tells you a great deal. Interest and hesitation are both useful information.
Repeated warmth over text combined with a consistent refusal to appear on
camera is the clearest warning sign there is — our guide to
[online dating safety]({{ '/online-dating-safety-tips-for-indian-singles/' | relative_url }})
goes through the rest.

Twenty minutes is plenty. You are checking two things: that they are who they
said they were, and that talking to them is enjoyable when it is not typed.

### Then suggest meeting, with something concrete

Vague plans die. "We should meet sometime" produces nothing; "there is a place
near the station that does decent coffee — Saturday afternoon?" produces an
answer either way.

Keep the first meeting short and public. A coffee that runs long is a good
outcome. A dinner that has to be endured is not.

this, you will be — the same principles apply over a longer timescale, plus a
few extra ones covered in our guide to

## When to stop

Not every conversation should be rescued. It is reasonable to let one go when:

- You are the only one asking questions, across several exchanges
- Plans keep being agreed to and then quietly dropped
- The tone has turned into something you have to manage
- You realise you are replying out of politeness rather than interest

Fading out slowly is the common approach and it is not the kind one. A short,
plain message — that you have enjoyed talking but do not think it is a match —
is easier to receive than a week of one-word answers, and it takes ten seconds.

## The short version

Open with something specific. Ask questions that cannot be answered in a word.
Give as much as you are given. Move to a call within a fortnight, and to a
meeting soon after. End things plainly when they are not working.

None of it is clever, and it is far more effective than anything that is.
