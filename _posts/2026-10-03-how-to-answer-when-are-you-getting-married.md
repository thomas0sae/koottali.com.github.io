Navratri, Durga Puja, Diwali, Christmas, and then the wedding season that
follows. Different festivals, different families, and the same question from
every relative within ten minutes of arriving: so, when are you getting married?

For NRI singles it might come on a video call home with half the family on
speaker, at a community Diwali event abroad, or in person on a trip back for a
cousin's wedding. The setting changes. The question does not.

It is almost never meant unkindly. It comes from habit, affection, and a
generation for whom marriage was the milestone that meant a life was on track.
That does not make it any less tiring the fifteenth time. A little preparation
makes it much easier to handle, and much easier to enjoy the rest of the season.

## Why the question keeps coming

It helps to understand what is behind it.

**It is the default small talk.** For many older relatives, asking about
marriage is what asking about the weather is for everyone else. It fills a gap.

**It is often about your parents.** Relatives may be asking on your parents'
behalf, or measuring your family against their own. Your answer is not really
the point.

**Distance fills gaps with guesses.** When you live abroad, relatives see you
once a year. In the absence of information, they assume: you are too focused on
work, too picky, secretly dating someone unsuitable. A calm, cheerful answer
replaces the guess.

**Comparison is in the air.** Festival season is when engagements get announced
and cousins arrive with new partners. Everyone is counting, whether they mean to
or not.

None of this is about you failing at anything. It is about other people's
expectations, which you are not obliged to meet on their timetable.

## Before the season: talk to your parents first

This is the single most useful thing you can do, and the easiest to skip.

Have a short conversation with your parents before the gatherings start:

- **Agree what can be shared.** Are you happy for them to say you are looking,
  that you are seeing someone, or nothing at all?
- **Ask them not to discuss it in front of others.** Many parents do not realise
  how it feels to be discussed across a room.
- **Give them a line.** "We've left that one to them. They'll tell us when
  there's news" ends most conversations, and gives your parents something to say
  that saves face for everyone.
- **Reassure them.** Parents often push hardest when they feel shut out. A
  little information in private buys a lot of peace in public.

## Answers that work

The best answer is short, true, warm and followed by a question back. You are not
trying to win. You are trying to move the conversation on pleasantly.

**The light deflection:** "When it happens, you'll be the first to know. Now,
tell me about your trip."

**The honest version:** "I'm looking, and I'd rather wait for the right person
than rush it."

**If you are seeing someone but not ready to share:** "Nothing to announce yet!"
It is true, and nobody can argue with it.

**With a little humour:** "I'm waiting for an auspicious muhurat." Older
relatives tend to enjoy this one.

**For the relative who asks every year:** "Aunty, I promise, when there's news,
you'll hear it from me."

**When it gets personal:** If the comments move on to your age, your weight or
what will happen if you "leave it too late", you are allowed to close it down:
"I know you mean well, but I'd rather not talk about it today." Then go and
refill your plate.

A few things help whichever line you use. Answer once, then change the subject.
Do not over-explain, because every detail you offer becomes a new question. And
keep your tone warm. A cheerful non-answer ends a conversation; a defensive one
starts a debate.

## When relatives bring biodatas

Festival season is also when matches get suggested. An aunty mentions a
colleague's son in Melbourne, or a printed biodata appears after dinner.

You do not have to decide anything on the spot. "Thank you, I'll have a look in
my own time" is polite and commits you to nothing. If you are open to family
introductions, they can be a perfectly good way to meet someone, alongside
everything else. Our post on the
[middle ground between arranged marriage and dating]({{ '/arranged-marriage-vs-dating-nri-middle-ground/' | relative_url }})
covers how to combine the two on your own terms.

If you do follow up, treat it like any other introduction: a conversation first,
then a video call, then a meeting when you are ready.

## Video calls home

For many NRIs, festival day itself happens on a screen: the family gathered
around a phone in someone's living room, everyone taking a turn.

A few things make these calls easier. Join after you have eaten, not hungry and
tired. Decide in advance roughly how long you will stay on. And have a natural
reason to leave ready: you are heading to a friend's place, or helping with the
cooking at your end. "I'll call you properly tomorrow, Amma" is a perfectly good
way to end a call that has taken a turn.

## Weddings, as a single guest

Wedding season can be the hardest stretch. You are surrounded by a celebration of
the exact thing everyone keeps asking you about.

Go anyway, and go for the right reasons: the family, the food and the dancing.
Bring a friend if a plus one is allowed. Spend time with the cousins and friends
you rarely see. And remember that weddings are, quietly, one of the oldest places
people meet. Plenty of couples were introduced at someone else's.

## Use the season for yourself

Festival season abroad is one of the best times of year to meet people, because
communities that are scattered for most of the year come together.

Diwali melas, garba and dandiya nights, Durga Puja celebrations, Christmas
services and New Year events draw people from across a city, and many of them
need volunteers. Helping to run something is a far easier way to meet people
than standing at the edge of the room. Our guide to

covers community events and other options.

## Look after yourself

- **Plan time with friends**, not just family. A dinner with people who do not ask
  about your marriage plans resets a lot.
- **Keep some of your own routine.** A run, a gym class, an hour with a book.
- **Mute generously.** Engagement announcements tend to arrive in clusters at
  this time of year, and it is fine to scroll past.
- **Skip things if you need to.** You do not have to attend every gathering to
  show that you care.

And if it gets heavy, remember that the question is about the asker's
expectations, not about your worth.

## The short version

Talk to your parents before the gatherings and agree what they will say. Have one
or two short, warm answers ready, give them once, and then ask a question back.
Take biodatas politely and decide later. Keep calls home to a length you can
enjoy, go to the weddings for the fun of it, and use community events to meet
people.

The question is coming either way. A little preparation means it takes ten
seconds rather than ten minutes.
