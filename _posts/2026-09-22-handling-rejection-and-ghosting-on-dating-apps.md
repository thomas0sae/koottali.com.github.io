Anyone using a dating app for more than a few weeks will experience some version
of this: a conversation that was going well, and then nothing. No argument, no
explanation, just a message that was never answered.

It stings more than it rationally should, and understanding why makes it much
easier to carry.

## What is actually happening

### Ghosting is usually avoidance, not judgement

The story people tell themselves is that the other person looked carefully at
them, weighed it up, and found them wanting.

That is almost never what happened. What happened is that someone lost interest,
felt awkward about saying so, and chose the option that required nothing of them.
Or they were talking to four people, the week got busy, and the conversation that
took the most effort was the one that lapsed. Or something entirely unrelated
happened in their life - a job, an ex, a family situation - that you will never
hear about and that had nothing to do with you.

None of that makes it pleasant. It does mean that reading it as a verdict is
reading far more into it than is there.

### Why it stings disproportionately

Two reasons.

The first is that silence has no information in it, so you supply your own - and
what people supply is almost always worse than the truth. A clear "this is not
working for me" is a small, closed wound. Silence stays open.

The second is volume. Dating apps compress something that used to happen a few
times a year into something that can happen weekly. The individual events are
small; the accumulation is what wears people down. That is a dosage problem, not
evidence about you.

## Keeping it in proportion

### Remember what they were judging

Someone who stops replying after a few messages is not rejecting you. They cannot
be - they do not know you.

They are responding to four photos, three lines of text, and a handful of
messages. That is a judgement made on almost no information, which is precisely
why it deserves almost no weight.

The corollary is worth sitting with: the people who matter in your life did not
decide about you in eleven seconds, and nobody who decides in eleven seconds was
going to be one of them.

### Keep the pool bigger than any one person

The most reliable protection against taking rejection badly is structural rather
than emotional.

If you have one conversation running and it goes quiet, that is your entire
dating life going quiet. If you have several, plus a community group, plus
friends who know you are looking, one silence is a minor event.

This is not about being cynical or keeping options open dishonestly. It is about
not letting a single stranger's Tuesday determine how you feel about yourself.

### Do not chase silence

One follow-up is fine, particularly if a plan had actually been made. Something
short and light, with no edge to it.

After that, treat silence as the answer, because it is. Messages sent into
silence do not recover a conversation - they make the other person more certain
and leave you feeling worse. The ten minutes you spend drafting the perfect
re-engagement message is the worst-value ten minutes in dating.

### Watch for the wrong conclusion

The damaging response is not sadness, it is generalisation. "That did not work"
is accurate. "Nobody here wants to date an Indian man," "I am too old for this,"
"apps are a waste of time" are all conclusions drawn from a sample far too small
to support them.

If you notice that pattern, it is usually a sign to step back for a fortnight,
not to try harder.

## When the pattern is real

Sometimes a run of silence is telling you something, and it is worth checking
before concluding the world is at fault.

**If you get very few matches at all**, the problem is the profile or the
filters, not rejection. A group photo first, an empty bio, and a 25km radius will
starve a profile regardless of who you are. Our post on

covers the fixes in order of impact.

**If conversations go well and then stop after a call or a first meeting**,
something in person is not landing the way the text version did. That is worth
honest reflection, ideally with a friend who will actually tell you.

## Be the person who says it plainly

Since everybody dislikes being ghosted, it is worth not doing it.

A short message costs nothing:

> I have enjoyed talking but I do not think we are a match. All the best.

No explanation is required, and offering one usually invites a negotiation. This
is kinder than a slow fade, and it takes less effort than the week of
diminishing one-word replies that most people choose instead.

The exception: if someone has been aggressive or made you uncomfortable, you owe
them nothing at all. Block and report, and do not treat politeness as an
obligation that outranks your own safety. Our post on

covers the rest.

## Protecting your appetite for it

A few habits that make a long search survivable:

- **Set a rhythm.** Twenty minutes a day beats two hours on a Sunday, which
  produces a burnout cycle.
- **Take breaks on purpose** rather than quitting in frustration. A planned
  fortnight off is restorative; a bitter one is not.
- **Do not review your own profile after a bad week.** You will make it worse.
- **Keep a life running alongside it.** People who are dating and nothing else
  feel every silence much more sharply.
- **Judge over months, not days.** Most of what happens in a given week is
  noise.

## The short version

Ghosting is avoidance, not assessment. Early rejection is a judgement made on
almost no information and deserves almost no weight. Keep several things going
so that no single silence is the whole picture, send one follow-up at most, and
notice the difference between bad luck and a fixable pattern.

And say it plainly when you are the one who is not interested - it takes ten
seconds and it is the version everyone wishes they had received.
